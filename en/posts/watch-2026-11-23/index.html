Cyber watch, week of 16 to 22 November 2026: Optimizely, vCenter, Java4Shell, Iran

Published 2026-11-23 · 8 min read

Optimizely compromised (178 sites affected), a vCenter flaw exploited since September, Java4Shell, France's official accusation against Iran, the Résilience bill adopted and a EUR 5 million CNIL fine.

Bulletin for the week of 16 to 22 November, published on 23 November 2026.

Optimizely compromised: 178 major websites served malicious code.

Key points

  1. A script provider, Optimizely, was compromised on 16 November: 178 major websites, including Ledger and the BBC, served for a day code that diverted cryptocurrency.
  2. Two major flaws to fix: VMware vCenter, secretly exploited since September by a China-linked group, and Java4Shell, which affects Java in many enterprise software products.
  3. France officially accused Iran of sabotaging supermarket fridges, and the National Assembly adopted the Résilience bill that transposes NIS2.
  4. The CNIL reminds everyone of the basics with a EUR 5 million fine for passwords stored in clear text.

This week’s topics

Optimizely compromised: malicious code injected into the sites of 178 organisations, including Ledger

Supply chain · Critical

What happened On 16 November, attackers exploited a flaw in Optimizely Experimentation, an A/B testing tool (comparing two versions of a page) used by large websites. They slipped a malicious script into the code served by Optimizely to 178 organisations, including Ledger, Zoom, the BBC, IKEA and HSBC. The script replaced crypto wallet addresses. It stayed active for a large part of the day before being cleaned up; Optimizely says it fixed the flaw and revoked access.

Why it matters The affected sites were not hacked themselves: a trusted provider served as the entry point. A single compromised provider was enough to reach hundreds of sites within hours. In GRC (governance, risk and compliance), it is the perfect example of the risk posed by third-party scripts loaded on a website.

Key takeaway If you paid by card on one of these sites on 16 November, watch your statements. Update your iPhone. For companies: inventory third-party scripts and limit what they can do (CSP policy, script integrity).

Sources: IT-Connect · Cyberattaque.org

VMware vCenter: a flaw rated 9.8 exploited since September by a China-linked group

Vulnerability · Critical

What happened Broadcom fixed on 19 November the flaw CVE-2026-123890 in vCenter, the console that manages VMware virtual servers. Without a password, an attacker can obtain a full administrator session. According to Mandiant (Google), the espionage group Armory Castle, suspected of being linked to China, has been exploiting it since early September. It first got in through another flaw (BeyondTrust), then moved laterally to vCenter and the ESXi servers.

Why it matters vCenter often controls all of a company’s virtual machines: losing it means losing the whole estate. Version 7.0, which is end of support, has no public fix. For a Blue Team (defence) role, you have to look for traces of intrusion, not just apply the patch.

Key takeaway Update vCenter (8.0 U3m, 9.0.2.0200 or 9.1.1.0200) without delay and never expose its console to the Internet. After a flaw exploited for months, fixing is not enough: you also have to check that you have not already been visited.

Sources: BleepingComputer · The Hacker News · SecurityWeek

Java4Shell: a Java flaw threatens Jira, Jenkins, Keycloak and many others

Vulnerability · Important

What happened Oracle and OpenJDK fixed on 17 November the flaw CVE-2026-114938, nicknamed Java4Shell. It lies in the Base64 decoding (a common format for carrying data) of Java versions 17, 21, 25 and 27. It can allow an application to be taken over, but only on certain recent processors (Intel Xeon since Ice Lake, AMD Zen 4). CERT-FR (the French government’s cyber alert and response centre) published an alert on 18 November; no successful attack was confirmed as of that date.

Why it matters Java is everywhere, often hidden in off-the-shelf software or containers. The researcher cites very widespread tools that are vulnerable without prior login, such as Jira, Confluence, Jenkins or Keycloak. The real work is the inventory: knowing where Java runs in your own environment.

Key takeaway Apply the Java updates (e.g. 17.0.21.1, 21.0.13.1) and those of the software that embeds Java. In the meantime, CERT-FR points to a Java startup option that disables the faulty code.

Sources: CERT-FR · SecurityWeek · Tenable

Sabotaged fridges: France officially accuses Iran

Espionage / State actors · Important · Follow-up

What happened Reminder: in October, the fridges of hundreds of supermarkets had been put into forced defrost remotely. On 9 November, the Quai d’Orsay (French foreign ministry) attributed the attack to the “Shahid Kaveh” unit, attached to Iran’s Revolutionary Guards, with a CERT-FR technical report published the same day. The official toll rises to more than 700 sites, at least 15 outbreaks of fire according to the press. France summoned Iran’s ambassador and will propose new European sanctions.

Why it matters Publicly attributing an attack to a state is rare and carries heavy diplomatic consequences. The report also shows that the targets were found by a simple search for equipment exposed on the Internet, with weak protections.

Key takeaway An industrial device (fridge, pump, controller) connected to the Internet with a default password can become a weapon. The reflex: never expose these interfaces directly and change the factory credentials.

Sources: France Diplomatie · INCYBER NEWS · L’EnerGeek

NIS2: the National Assembly adopts the Résilience bill by a large majority

Regulation · Important · Follow-up

What happened On 17 November, members of Parliament adopted the Résilience bill, which transposes the NIS2 directive, by 464 votes to 64. The adopted text differs from the Senate’s: a joint committee (commission mixte paritaire, CMP) can now be convened to find a common version. Promulgation, implementing decrees and the ANSSI reference framework (ANSSI is the French national cybersecurity agency) will follow.

Why it matters Thousands of companies and local authorities (around 15,000 according to several estimates) will have to manage their cyber risks and report their incidents. It is a large source of GRC jobs for the coming years.

Key takeaway The obligations will only apply after the decrees, but the companies concerned can already start: inventory, risk analysis, incident reporting procedure (alert within 24 hours, notification within 72 hours).

Sources: Leto · Next

Passwords stored in clear text: the CNIL imposes a EUR 5 million fine

Regulation · Important

What happened On 18 November, the CNIL (the French data protection authority) published a EUR 5 million penalty against a company it does not name; according to the press, Macif confirmed it is the one targeted. The passwords of customer accounts were kept in clear text, meaning readable as they are. Access accounts were also shared between several employees, and some data was kept far too long (more than 10 years).

Why it matters These are basic security rules, and the CNIL penalises them heavily even without a known breach. For a GRC role, it is a good example of what an audit checks: password storage, named accounts, retention periods.

Key takeaway A password must never be stored in readable form: only its fingerprint (hash) is kept. Each employee must have their own account, so that you know who did what.

Sources: CNIL · Refrance

N-able: intrusion on the servers of its email filtering service

Attack · Watch · Follow-up

What happened Reminder: N-able, a publisher of tools for IT service providers, was already dealing with exploited N-central flaws. This week, it confirmed unauthorised access to a server of Mail Assure and SpamExperts, its email filtering services, after first speaking of a simple technical problem. Emails still go through, but dashboards, the API and quarantine are cut off in the Americas, Asia and Europe. N-able says this incident is unrelated to N-central, whose hosted versions it shut down to fix more than 30 flaws.

Why it matters When a provider’s tool is compromised, all of its customers are exposed at once. This type of supplier is a prime target.

Key takeaway If you use these services: follow N-able’s status page and prepare to change your administration credentials. A communication that shifts from “outage” to “intrusion” is common: you have to follow the updates.

Sources: BleepingComputer

Bordeaux University Hospital: a ransomware group claims a theft of medical records

Ransomware · Watch

What happened On 22 November, the group Scarlettgroup, which appeared in October, published on its site a claim targeting the CHU de Bordeaux (Bordeaux University Hospital). It says it holds patient data (social security numbers, medical records, test results) and internal documents, with a countdown of about seven days. No sample has been verified and the hospital has not confirmed an intrusion: this is an unconfirmed claim.

Why it matters Hospitals remain priority targets because their data is highly sensitive. Many groups now steal data without encrypting systems, to apply pressure through the threat of publication.

Key takeaway A claim is not proof: wait for a verified sample or a confirmation from the victim. On the defence side, monitoring large outbound data transfers helps detect this type of theft.

Sources: Cyberattaque.org

Bell Canada says it was hacked by an attacker using AI agents

AI & cyber · Watch

What happened Canadian operator Bell announced that an “agentic” attacker, meaning one relying on autonomous AI tools, stole business customer data on 17 November. The attacker came in through a pre-production environment (a test system) used to order telecom lines. Names, job titles, business emails and phone numbers, ticket titles and a few IP addresses leaked; Bell says it contained the incident, with no impact on services.

Why it matters Companies are starting to publicly describe attacks carried out with AI agents, which move faster. Entry through a test system also shows that these environments are often less well protected than production.

Key takeaway A test environment often contains real data: it must be protected like production, or filled with fake data.

Sources: BleepingComputer

Glossary

  • Skimmer: code hidden in a web page that copies the bank card numbers typed into a payment form.
  • Attribution: publicly naming the author of a cyberattack (group, state), based on technical and intelligence evidence.
  • Hypervisor: software that runs several virtual machines on a single physical server (e.g. VMware ESXi, managed by vCenter).
  • Password hashing: one-way transformation of a password into a fingerprint: the site can verify the password without storing it in readable form.
  • Pre-production environment: a copy of a system used for testing before going live; often less protected, even though it sometimes contains real data.
