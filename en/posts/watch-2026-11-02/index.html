Binance: $6.3 billion stolen by North Korea

Published 2026-11-02 · 9 min read

Weekly bulletin for 27 October to 1 November 2026: record Binance theft, exploited flaws (PHP2Shell, GitLab, Brotli, Cisco, Ivanti), political party leaks, the Belgian tax authority shutdown and a CNIL fine against Fnac.

Weekly bulletin, week of 27 October to 1 November 2026.

The essentials

  1. The largest cryptocurrency theft in history: $6.3 billion stolen from Binance, attributed by the FBI to the North Korean group TraderTraitor.
  2. October’s vulnerabilities turn into real attacks: PHP2Shell compromises Palo Alto firewalls, and GitLab, Brotli and a new Cisco flaw are confirmed as exploited.
  3. In France, the wave of leaks hitting political parties continues (PRG, Écologistes, RN) and a second teenager is indicted over the tax-authority hack.
  4. Belgium shuts down its tax authority’s online services after a cyberattack, and the CNIL (the French data protection authority) fines Fnac €7 million for trackers set without consent.

Binance: $6.3 billion stolen, the biggest theft in history

Attack · Critical

What happened: on 29 October around 20:05 UTC, attackers drained “hot” wallets (permanently connected) belonging to Binance, the largest cryptocurrency platform. About $6.3 billion was taken ($6.43 billion according to Elliptic), the largest theft ever recorded. Binance first pointed to a compromised computer belonging to a DevOps engineer, then to other entry points. On 31 October, the FBI attributed the theft to the North Korean group TraderTraitor.

Why it matters: nearly $3 billion could be frozen, because “stablecoins” (USDT, USDC) have an issuer able to block funds; bitcoin cannot be frozen. The starting point is the workstation of a single technical employee, a classic North Korean recipe. For the Blue Team, administrators’ machines are priority targets to monitor.

Key takeaway: high-privilege accounts and workstations (DevOps, administrators) must be isolated, hardened and monitored first. Binance reopened withdrawals in stages on 1 November and says it is covering the losses. After this kind of affair, beware of fake “fund recovery services”.

Sources: TechCrunch · Elliptic · CoinDesk

PHP2Shell: Palo Alto firewalls compromised, real intrusions observed

Vulnerability · Critical · Follow-up

What happened: reminder: these PHP flaws have been patched since 21 October. This week, Palo Alto confirmed exploitation of the management interfaces of its firewalls exposed to the Internet and released PAN-OS fixes. The company Dream describes a campaign cautiously attributed to a China-linked actor: 240 firewalls targeted, 24 compromised (United States, Japan, Taiwan, South Korea), including two on a network linked to the US government, combining PHP2Shell with the Linux flaw Fragnesia (CVE-2026-46300, added to KEV on 28/10). GreyNoise observed at least six successful exploitations, with credential theft and an SSH key left behind to return.

Why it matters: a flaw in a programming language also affects products where you would not think of it, such as a firewall. In one week, we went from simple scanning to “hands-on” intrusions.

Key takeaway: an administration interface must never be open to the Internet: restrict it to internal addresses. If you patched late, also look for traces (added SSH keys, web shells): a patch does not evict an intruder who is already inside.

Sources: The Hacker News · The Record · PwnDefend

GitLab: flaw CVE-2026-97229 (10/10) is exploited

Vulnerability · Critical · Follow-up

What happened: reminder: GitLab keeps stringing together critical flaws tied to its regular-expression engine. The previous one, CVE-2026-97229 (patched on 12/10), is now exploited: CISA added it to KEV on 29/10, with a deadline of 1 November. Alpine Linux suffered attempts on 20-21 then 25-26/10 against its unpatched GitLab: eight crashes, no evidence of success, but the instance was rebuilt from scratch.

Why it matters: a GitLab server holds source code and often deployment secrets: breaking into it means being able to booby-trap software. Alpine shows the right reaction when a compromise cannot be ruled out.

Key takeaway: an exposed self-hosted GitLab must be updated without delay. When in doubt about a compromise, rebuilding cleanly is better than “cleaning up”.

Sources: The Hacker News · CISA

Political parties: PRG, Écologistes and RN hit in turn

Data leak · Important · Follow-up

What happened: the series continues. On 30/10, ChimeraZ released a file attributed to the Parti radical de gauche (825 records with addresses, phone numbers, birth dates, occupations), then on 31/10 a small file attributed to the Écologistes (125 records, mostly names and emails). On 31/10, another unnamed actor published a 150,000-line extract (about 121,300 people) presented as the RN’s member file, while announcing 432,597 records. None of these parties had confirmed as of 31/10.

Why it matters: party membership reveals a political opinion, a “sensitive” data category under the GDPR. These files are then used for targeted scams or harassment. In GRC, it is a reminder: you need to know which providers (member-management software) process your data.

Key takeaway: a claim is not proof: the figures announced by attackers are often inflated. If you are a party member, beware of messages that play on your commitment to get you to click.

Sources: Fuites Infos · Cyberattaque.org (PRG) · Cyberattaque.org (Écologistes)

Cisco: an ASA/FTD firewall flaw exploited to cut off VPNs

Vulnerability · Important

What happened: Cisco patched CVE-2026-76418 (score 8.6) in its ASA and FTD firewalls, already exploited by unknown attackers. Without a password, a booby-trapped request during an SSL VPN connection makes the device reboot. Only configurations with SSL VPN and “secondary authentication” are affected. CISA added it to KEV on 29/10; Shadowserver counts more than 51,000 exposed ASA VPNs.

Why it matters: it is a denial of service: nothing is stolen, but a VPN rebooting in a loop cuts off all remote work. Network-edge devices remain attackers’ favorite target.

Key takeaway: update to a fixed version. In the meantime, disable secondary authentication on the affected VPN tunnel groups.

Sources: BleepingComputer · The Hacker News

Ivanti Connect Secure: the last two links of the chain are patched

Patches · Important · Follow-up

What happened: reminder: a chain of Ivanti VPN flaws has been exploited since 3 October. On 29/10, Ivanti patched the two remaining links: CVE-2026-111357 (8.8, privilege escalation to root) and CVE-2026-118527 (4.3, predictable file names). Versions 25.1.1.3 and 22.8R2.6 fix all three flaws; CISA added them to KEV on 30/10. According to Volexity, the attacker (UTA0576) erased its traces in the logs and went after administrator passwords and BitLocker keys.

Why it matters: an appliance that was “up to date” on 3 October was still hacked: the original patch was not enough. The attacker leaves no file behind, which makes the investigation difficult.

Key takeaway: install 25.1.1.3 or 22.8R2.6. When in doubt, take a copy of memory and disk for analysis, then change the LAPS passwords and the BitLocker keys accessible to the service account.

Sources: Cybersecurity News · Sherlock Forensics (weekly recap)

Brotli (Node2Shell): exploitation confirmed

Vulnerability · Important · Follow-up

What happened: reminder: this flaw in the Brotli decoder affects Node.js, .NET, PHP and many others. On 27/10, CISA added it to KEV, which confirms real attacks, with no details published on the victims. US federal agencies have until 10 November.

Why it matters: Brotli is a compression building block hidden in thousands of pieces of software: many teams do not even know they use it. That is the whole point of a component inventory (SBOM).

Key takeaway: after updating, restart processes and rebuild container images. If that is not possible, block Brotli-compressed requests at the proxy.

Sources: BleepingComputer

Belgium: the tax authority shuts down its online services after a cyberattack

Attack · Important

What happened: on 28 October, the SPF Finances (the Belgian tax administration) announced a cyberattack. MyMinfin and other digital services were shut down as a precaution. At this stage, nothing indicates data theft. Extra time has been granted for some tax returns.

Why it matters: deliberately cutting your services is “containment”: a classic incident-response step to stop the attacker going further. The price is inconvenience for users, hence the importance of a continuity plan.

Key takeaway: cutting quickly is better than letting an intruder settle in. An organization must plan in advance how to operate without its online services and how to announce it.

Sources: La Libre Belgique

Tax-authority hack: a second teenager is indicted

Justice and law enforcement · Important

What happened: at the end of October, a 16-year-old (15 at the time of the events) was indicted (mis en examen) in Paris over the summer 2026 hack of the tax authority, as part of an organized gang, and placed under judicial supervision. He was arrested again after analysis of the equipment seized during a police custody in September. He allegedly acted with an 18-year-old accomplice nicknamed “ChatNoir”; both are suspected of belonging to the Zerobytes group. The article mentions 678,000 taxpayers affected.

Why it matters: according to the report by ANSSI (the French national cybersecurity agency) published in September, it was not a sophisticated attack: credentials stolen by spyware on unmanaged PCs, and a session left open after the password change, which allowed 16 more hours of theft.

Key takeaway: when an account is hacked, changing the password is not enough: you must also close all open sessions. And two-factor authentication must cover all portals, not just the main ones.

Sources: Génération NT · Cyberattaque.org (ANSSI report)

CNIL: €7 million fine for the Fnac app

Regulation · To watch

What happened: the CNIL made public on 29/10 a €7 million penalty (€6 million for Fnac Darty Participations et Services, €1 million for Fnac Direct). The mobile app sent a third party an identifier and the products viewed without consent, even when the user refused. The case stems from a complaint by the association noyb. Fnac must come into compliance within one month, or face €10,000 per day of delay.

Why it matters: consent to trackers is not only about websites: mobile apps are also inspected. A “refuse” button that blocks nothing is expensive.

Key takeaway: in GRC, the banner alone is not enough: you check technically what the app really sends, before and after a refusal.

Sources: CNIL · Next

WhatsApp: the “vote for a friend’s child” scam is back

Scams · To watch

What happened: a contact (whose account is already hacked) asks you to vote for a child in a contest. The link leads to a fake page that asks for the 6-digit code sent by SMS by WhatsApp. By giving it, you hand your account to the scammer, who then forwards the message to all your contacts.

Why it matters: the scam works because it comes from someone close and seems harmless. The stolen account is then used to ask your contacts for money.

Key takeaway: never type on a web page a code received by SMS. Enable two-step verification (PIN code) in WhatsApp and check “Linked devices”.

Sources: Selectra · DH (AFP)

Glossary

  • Hot wallet: a cryptocurrency wallet permanently connected to the Internet to process withdrawals quickly. Convenient, but more exposed than an offline “cold” wallet.
  • Denial of service (DoS): an attack that makes a service unavailable (crash, reboot, saturation) without necessarily stealing data. DDoS is its distributed version, launched from many machines.
  • Privilege escalation: a technique that lets an attacker already on a system obtain more rights, up to administrator or root.
  • Management interface: a web page or API used to administer a device (firewall, router). It must remain reachable only from the internal network.
  • Mise en examen (indictment): a decision by an investigating judge who considers there is serious evidence against a person. It is not a conviction: the person remains presumed innocent.
