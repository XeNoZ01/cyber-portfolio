*Daily bulletin of 27 October 2026.*

## The essentials

1. In France, a file attributed to Actil, which handles third-party payment (tiers payant) for hearing aids, allegedly exposes the health data and social security numbers of about 61,000 policyholders, mostly elderly; the leak is not confirmed.
2. In Japan, GMO refuses a $50 million ransom after up to 26,739 virtual desktops were hacked, while in Europe fake "IT support" calls on Teams open the door to ransomware.
3. PHP2Shell enters CISA's KEV catalog, but Unit 42 withdraws the European intrusion case it had attributed to it.

### Actil: a file on 61,000 hearing-aid policyholders released for free

**Data leak · Important**

**What happened**: on 26 October, a file presented as Actil's database was made freely available for download on a hacker forum. Actil, based in Lyon, handles third-party payment (the policyholder does not advance the reimbursed share) for hearing aids, on behalf of supplementary insurers such as Apicil and Alptis. About 61,000 people would be affected, nearly nine in ten of them over 60. At the time of writing the leak was unconfirmed; one source says Actil later confirmed a cyberattack on 27 October and interrupted its services.

**Why it matters**: the file reportedly contains the social security number, the insurer, the hearing aid, the amounts reimbursed and even a blindness indicator: this is health data, the most protected category under the GDPR. The victims are mostly elderly people, a favorite target of phone scams. In GRC (governance, risk and compliance), this is a textbook example of third-party risk, where a provider handles sensitive data.

**Key takeaway**: a claimed leak is not yet a confirmed leak, but the sample is enough for credible scams. If an elderly relative wears a hearing aid, warn them: no insurer asks for bank details over the phone. In a company, controlling subcontractors (GDPR Article 28) is part of the GRC job.

*Sources: Fuites Infos*

### Japan: 26,739 virtual desktop servers hacked at GMO, $50 million ransom refused

**Attack · Important**

**What happened**: GMO Internet acknowledged that an attacker had installed malicious programs on the servers of customers of its Onamae.com Desktop Cloud service, virtual desktops (VDI) used for foreign-exchange trading. Up to 26,739 servers may be affected, customer data was exfiltrated and some customers saw unknown transactions on their accounts. The attacker reportedly demands $50 million; GMO refuses to pay, will not restart the platform and offers six months of a replacement environment.

**Why it matters**: the CEO admits that the company's own ethical hackers had flagged weaknesses, but that the company did not react fast enough. It is a governance lesson: a known, untreated risk ends up costing a great deal. The CEO also mentions the AI tool ARTEX in another series of intrusions targeting about twenty Japanese companies, with no established link to this attack.

**Key takeaway**: when a password is stolen, changing it is not enough: you must also close all open sessions, because some apps stay logged in. And an internal audit that finds flaws is useless without a remediation plan followed by management.

*Sources: The Register*

### Microsoft Teams: fake "IT services" trap employees

**Scams · Important**

**What happened**: Arctic Wolf has identified hundreds of cases where attackers (tracked as STAC4749) first flood an employee's mailbox with newsletter sign-ups. They then contact the employee through Teams' external chat, posing as IT support that will "fix the problem". The victim then installs a remote-access tool (Quick Assist, AnyDesk, ScreenConnect…), after which the attackers explore the network, steal data and, in some cases, deploy the Chaos ransomware (according to Sophos).

**Why it matters**: the attack exploits no vulnerability: it exploits trust and a Teams setting left open to outside contacts. For the Blue Team, workstations and identities must be monitored, not just messages. .onmicrosoft.com domains (or, according to some reports, .top domains) are often the starting point: a clue to watch for.

**Key takeaway**: the real IT department almost never contacts you out of the blue right after a flood of emails. On the company side: restrict external Teams chat to known partners and alert on any unauthorized remote-access tool.

*Sources: The Hacker News*

### Australia takes over an Amadey network and frees 180,000 computers

**Justice and law enforcement · To watch**

**What happened**: Australia's cyber intelligence agency (ASD) infiltrated the command server of an Amadey malware operator, then disconnected it from its victims. About 180,000 infected computers worldwide were involved. In another case, an ASD tip concerned 5 TB of stolen corporate data put up for sale.

**Why it matters**: Amadey is a "loader" sold to criminals since 2018: it serves as an entry point to install other malware. Cutting the server stops the infection, but does not give back the passwords and cookies already stolen. Police are increasingly going on the offensive against criminals' infrastructure.

**Key takeaway**: a police cleanup does not replace remediation: after an infection, change passwords and revoke sessions, because a stolen cookie can bypass two-factor authentication.

*Sources: The Hacker News*

### PHP2Shell: CISA confirms exploitation, Unit 42 withdraws its intrusion example

**Vulnerability · Critical · Follow-up**

**What happened**: reminder: two critical PHP flaws were patched on 21 October. On 26 October, CISA added them to its KEV catalog of exploited vulnerabilities and gave US federal agencies until Thursday 29 October to fix them. Conversely, Unit 42 (Palo Alto) withdrew its claim that a European administration had been hacked via PHP2Shell: it now speaks of a "vulnerable PHP application" without being able to confirm these CVEs.

**Why it matters**: KEV listing is a strong signal: it is the list many teams use to prioritize patches. Unit 42's withdrawal shows that attributing an intrusion to a specific flaw can be revised; yesterday's bulletin presented this case as linked to PHP2Shell, and that link is no longer confirmed.

**Key takeaway**: always date and source your claims in a report, and be ready to correct them. For PHP: if your servers are not yet up to date, this is now an official emergency.

*Sources: BleepingComputer · The Hacker News*

### Brotli: PHP republishes its Windows packages

**Patches · To watch · Follow-up**

**What happened**: reminder: CVE-2026-111497 is a memory corruption in the Brotli compression library. On 26 October, PHP rebuilt its official Windows packages (8.5.12-1, 8.4.27-1, 8.3.36-1, 8.2.35-1), which shipped the vulnerable Brotli 1.2.0. The flaw is reachable through the cURL extension when it automatically decompresses responses, which the Guzzle library does by default.

**Why it matters**: the php -v command shows the same version before and after: only the file's "-1" suffix and its SHA-256 hash make it possible to check. It is a classic trap in version inventories.

**Key takeaway**: to verify a patch, do not rely on the version number alone: compare the file's hash or the list of components (SBOM).

*Sources: The Hacker News*

## Glossary

- **KEV catalog**: "Known Exploited Vulnerabilities", a public list from CISA (the US cyber agency) of flaws with proven real-world exploitation. Many teams use it to decide what to fix first.
- **Virtual desktop (VDI)**: a Windows or Linux computer running on a remote server: the user connects to it over the Internet. If the server is hacked, every desktop it hosts is too.
- **Email bombing**: flooding a mailbox with thousands of messages (often newsletter sign-ups) to drown real alerts or set up a fake offer of help.
- **Loader**: a small piece of malware whose job is to install itself quietly, then download and run other malware (password stealers, ransomware).
