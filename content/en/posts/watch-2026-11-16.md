*Bulletin for the week of 9 to 15 November, published on 16 November 2026.*

## Key points

1. The biggest leak of the week comes from a subcontractor: Maileva (La Poste) announces the theft of data on 22 million mail recipients.
2. On the vulnerability side, N-able N-central is being compromised even on up-to-date servers, Barracuda confirms intrusions, and Microsoft patches an Outlook flaw that is already being exploited.
3. In France, the National Assembly rewrote the NIS2 transposition law (final vote on 17 November) and the CNIL fined ten local authorities over their cybersecurity.
4. The SGS vehicle inspection networks confirm their incident: beware of fake fine text messages.

## This week's topics

### Maileva (La Poste): data on 22 million people stolen

**Data leak · Critical**

**What happened**
Maileva, a mail-sending service for businesses (Docaposte, La Poste group), announced this week a data theft affecting around 22 million people. The service had been shut down on 23 September after a cyberattack. Those affected are mostly recipients of mail sent by its customers: social landlords, laboratories, hospitals, transport operators. Names, first names and contact details are involved, sometimes with invoice amounts, and some letters may have been read. The French cybercrime unit has been brought in.

**Why it matters**
This is one of the biggest French leaks of the year, and it goes through a subcontractor: the victims have often never heard of Maileva. In GRC (governance, risk and compliance), it is the textbook case of supplier risk: a company remains responsible for the data it entrusts to a provider.

**Key takeaway**
Expect fake letters or text messages that mention your landlord, your lab or a real invoice: always check through the official website. For companies, a list of their subcontractors and of what each one holds is the basis of good risk management.

*Sources: franceinfo · Clubic*

### N-able N-central: a flaw exploited even on up-to-date servers

**Vulnerability · Critical**

**What happened**
N-central is a remote management tool widely used by IT service providers (MSPs). On 12 November, a researcher published an exploit, "4EverAble2Shell", that gives full (root) control of a server without a password, even with the latest patch of 10 November. Compromises were reported a few hours later, with a cryptocurrency miner being installed; one researcher links 21 compromised servers to the attacker, one of them in France. Only servers with the old "Legacy API" enabled and exposed to the Internet are affected.

**Why it matters**
Compromising a provider's tool means being able to reach all of its customers at once. It is also a reminder that "patched" does not mean "protected" when a risky feature is left open.

**Key takeaway**
Disable N-central's Legacy API right away, as N-able asks. An exposed server should be treated as compromised: look for an XMRig miner and for unknown startup tasks (systemd, cron).

*Sources: SecurityWeek · The Hacker News*

### Monthly updates: exploited Outlook flaw and Chrome's 8th zero-day

**Patches · Important**

**What happened**
Microsoft's Patch Tuesday of 10 November fixes about 1,100 flaws (excluding Edge). Only one is already being exploited: CVE-2026-124194 in Outlook, which makes Outlook connect to an attacker's server when a booby-trapped Office file is opened, which can leak the hash of the Windows password (NTLMv2). Other serious flaws affect LDAP, Windows Search and Outlook's reading pane. On 9 November, Google also fixed CVE-2026-125350, an already exploited flaw in Chrome's XML component, the eighth of the year.

**Why it matters**
The flaws in Outlook's reading pane and in Windows Search trigger with no click, just by receiving an email. For a Blue Team (defence), Patch Tuesday sets the monthly rhythm of patch management.

**Key takeaway**
Install the November Windows and Office updates, and update Chrome to version 157.0.8103.50 or later (restart the browser). Blocking outbound SMB traffic to the Internet limits NTLM hash theft.

*Sources: IT-Connect · SecurityWeek*

### The CNIL sanctions ten local authorities for poor cybersecurity

**Regulation · Important**

**What happened**
The CNIL (the French data protection authority) announced on 9 November ten penalties against local authorities and one public body, with fines from €3,000 to €20,000. The criticisms: no two-factor authentication for administrators, service providers permanently connected, unmonitored logs, a poorly segmented network, obsolete servers and weak passwords. Some had not carried out an impact assessment for their school online services or their video surveillance. The authorities are not named.

**Why it matters**
This list is a genuine reference for the most common mistakes, useful for an audit. It shows that the GDPR also penalises a lack of security, not only the use made of data.

**Key takeaway**
MFA for privileged accounts, time-limited access for service providers and logs that are actually read: these are the first three points to check in a security audit.

*Sources: CNIL*

### NIS2: the National Assembly examined the Résilience bill, final vote on 17 November

**Regulation · Important · Follow-up**

**What happened**
Members of Parliament examined the text on 9 and 10 November and largely rewrote it, sometimes against the government's opinion. Article 16 bis, which prohibited imposing backdoors in encryption, was removed by 69 votes to 37. Additions include a single incident notification (ANSSI, the French national cybersecurity agency, CNIL, ACPR, AMF), e-invoicing platforms classed as essential entities, and cyber risk in the municipal safeguard plan. The formal vote takes place on 17 November, followed by a joint committee (commission mixte paritaire).

**Why it matters**
NIS2 will create obligations for thousands of entities in France: it is a major driver of GRC hiring. The planned fines go up to €10M or 2% of worldwide turnover for essential entities.

**Key takeaway**
The text may still change in the joint committee, and decrees will then be needed. Follow above all the list of sectors and the incident notification procedure.

*Sources: Journal du Net · Leto*

### Fortinet finally discloses the 8 critical flaws fixed in October

**Patches · Important · Follow-up**

**What happened**
As announced, Fortinet published on 10 November the details of the fixes it released quietly on 21 and 22 October. They concern 8 critical flaws (up to 9.8 out of 10) in FortiOS, FortiManager, FortiAnalyzer and FortiSASE, six of them requiring no authentication. No exploitation is confirmed, but Fortinet states that working code exists for some of them.

**Why it matters**
Now that the details are public, attackers can compare versions to build attacks. Fortinet devices are often targeted right after this kind of publication.

**Key takeaway**
Check that your FortiGate and FortiManager devices are on the October versions (e.g. FortiOS 7.4.13 or 7.6.8). Disable the JSON API if it is not needed and do not leave administration reachable from the Internet.

*Sources: Cybernews · Daily CyberSecurity*

### Barracuda: email gateways compromised through two chained flaws

**Vulnerability · Important**

**What happened**
Barracuda confirmed on 9 November that a few organisations were compromised through its email security gateways (ESG). Two flaws are chained: CVE-2026-116606 (authentication bypass, 9.3) and CVE-2026-111560 (command injection, 8.6). Together, they give full control of the device without a password if its administration interface is reachable. An exploit had been public since 3 November.

**Why it matters**
An email gateway sees all of a company's mail go through: controlling it allows messages to be spied on or tampered with. Barracuda ESG had already been massively targeted by Chinese spies in 2023.

**Key takeaway**
Move to ESG 9.4.0.030 or later and close Internet access to the administration interface. If it was exposed, look for traces of intrusion before considering the device clean.

*Sources: Daily CyberSecurity*

### Maersk confirms an intrusion claimed by the WhiteArmor group

**Ransomware · Important**

**What happened**
Shipping giant Maersk acknowledged on 11 November unauthorised access and a data leak on an old system inherited from an acquisition. The WhiteArmor group, which had listed Maersk on its site on 7 and 8 November, demands $5 million and says it holds 100 GB of data from LF Logistics (acquired in 2022). Maersk states that no sensitive data appears to be exposed and that there is no impact on its operations.

**Why it matters**
Systems taken over in an acquisition are often forgotten and less well protected. In GRC, integrating the security of an acquired company is part of risk management.

**Key takeaway**
After an acquisition, inventory and bring up to standard (or shut down) the legacy systems. A hacker claim still has to be verified, but an admission of intrusion by the company makes it credible.

*Sources: IT-Connect*

### Vehicle inspection: Sécuritest, Auto Sécurité and Vérif'Autos confirm the incident

**Data leak · Important · Follow-up**

**What happened**
The three vehicle inspection networks of the SGS group warned their customers on 14 November of a cybersecurity incident affecting their online appointment booking tools. According to them, the exposed data are: last name, first name, address, email, phone number and licence plate, with no banking data. They give no figures: the link with the database of 22.7 million people put up for sale has not been established.

**Why it matters**
Linking a name to a licence plate and a phone number enables very convincing scams (fake fine, fake inspection reminder).

**Key takeaway**
Be wary of text messages about fines or a "inspection to redo": pay only on antai.gouv.fr or the centre's official website.

*Sources: Fuites Infos*

### Binance theft: US justice seizes $1.2 billion

**Espionage / State actors · Watch · Follow-up**

**What happened**
On 12 November, the US Department of Justice obtained the right to seize or freeze more than $1.2 billion from the theft, out of more than $2.7 billion frozen in total. It also seized the infrastructure of "FaceStealer", another North Korean campaign: fake video calls with videos or deepfakes of acquaintances pushed victims to install malware (more than $400M stolen in 18 months). Binance says it has finished covering all the affected assets.

**Why it matters**
Fake recruitment and fake video calls have become North Korean hackers' favourite entry points, against companies as well as individuals.

**Key takeaway**
A "technical problem" in a video call that requires installing software is a warning sign. Hang up and call the person back through another channel.

*Sources: The Block*

### 1001RDV: medical appointment booking, a leak claimed and denied

**Data leak · Watch**

**What happened**
On 12 November, the website of 1001RDV, a medical appointment booking and remote secretarial tool, was replaced by a message from hackers saying they hold the data of 9 million patients. The publisher Logicielnet denies it: only the WordPress showcase site was allegedly affected. But on 14 November, data presented as coming from 1001RDV was put up for sale; the sample contains patient identities and reasons for appointments.

**Why it matters**
Health data is among the most sensitive and the most resold. A quick denial can itself be contradicted by the facts: this is a key point of crisis communication.

**Key takeaway**
The leak is not confirmed at this stage. A company must verify before stating "no data affected": a compromised showcase site can hide more.

*Sources: Fuites Infos*

## Glossary

- **Multi-factor authentication (MFA)**: a login that asks for a second proof in addition to the password (code, app, physical key). It blocks most password thefts.
- **Processor / subcontractor (under the GDPR)**: a company that processes data on behalf of another (e.g. Maileva sending mail for a landlord). The customer remains responsible and must govern this provider by contract.
- **DPIA (data protection impact assessment)**: a mandatory study before high-risk data processing (video surveillance, children's data, etc.) to assess the risks and plan protections.
- **Proof of concept (PoC)**: code that demonstrates that a flaw is exploitable. Once published, attacks often follow within hours.
- **Double extortion**: attackers steal the data and then threaten to publish it if the ransom is not paid, in addition to (or instead of) encrypting the files.
